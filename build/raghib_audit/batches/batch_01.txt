ROOT بله   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) of the root بله is a state of mental deficiency or heedlessness, specifically an incapacity to discern or manage evil (الغفلة عن الشرّ وعدم إحسانه). It denotes a congenital or acquired deficiency in practical intelligence, not a moral failing.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning according to Asās al-Balāghah is a person who is simple-minded, naive, or foolish, particularly one who is unable to recognize or deal with harm. It is used literally for a deficient intellect, as in 'خير أولادنا الأبله العقول' (the best of our children are those with simple minds) and 'خير النساء البلهاء الخجول' (the best of women is the naive, modest one). The verb forms include بَلِهَ (baliha) and تَبَالَهَ (tabālaha), meaning to feign foolishness or to act naive.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorically and scholastically, the root extends to: (1) a praiseworthy simplicity or innocence, especially in women, where lack of cunning is a virtue; (2) feigned foolishness (تباله) as a rhetorical or social strategy, as in the verse by ʿUmar ibn Abī Rabīʿa where women pretend not to recognize the poet; (3) in the phrase 'بله ما أضمره' (bale mā aḍmara-hu), it functions as an imperative or interjection meaning 'leave aside' or 'never mind' what he concealed, indicating a dismissal or a transition to a better alternative. This usage is metaphorical, moving from the concrete sense of mental deficiency to a pragmatic particle of exception or disregard.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: البَلَهُ: الغَفْلة عن الشرّ وأَن لا يُحْسِنَهُ؛ بَلِهَ، بالكسر، بَلَهاً وتَبَلَّه وهو أَبْلَه وابتُلِهَ كبَلِه.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Al-balah is heedlessness of evil and the inability to manage it; baliha (with kasra) yields balahan, and taballah is to affect it; one is ablah, and ibtuliha is like baliha.

ROOT بنط   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The triliteral sequence ب-ن-ط is classified by the classical lexicographers as a root that is 'muhmal' (unused/neglected) in its bare form; its ontological origin is therefore not an independent physical act or entity, but rather a dialectal Yemeni nominal designation for a practitioner of weaving, realized only when the sequence is augmented by an intervening letter.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is restricted to the Yemeni dialectal noun 'al-biyanṭ' (البِيَنْطُ), denoting a weaver (al-nassāj). This is a concrete occupational designation, not a verbal root, and its morphological pattern corresponds to 'al-biyaṭr' (البِيَطْرُ), indicating that the word is a quadriliteral or augmented nominal form rather than a triliteral verb.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: No metaphorical, philosophical, or theological usage is attested. The root is neither employed in Quranic discourse nor in speculative theology (kalām) or philosophy (falsafa). Its only extension is the dialectal occupational noun for a weaver; any attempt to derive abstract or scholastic meanings would be an illegitimate projection beyond the classical lexical evidence.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: قال الأزهري: أَما بنط فهو مهمل، فإِذا فصل بين الباء والنون بياء كان مستعملاً، يقول أَهل اليمن للنّسَّاج البِيَنْطُ، وعلى وزنه البِيَطْرُ.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Al-Azhari said: As for 'bnṭ', it is unused (muhmal); but when a yāʾ separates the bāʾ and the nūn, it becomes employed. The people of Yemen call the weaver 'al-biyanṭ', and it follows the pattern of 'al-biyaṭr'.

ROOT بنق   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) centers on the act of gathering, tightening, and securing disparate elements into a coherent, bounded whole — specifically, the stitching or fastening of garment seams and the binding of a book's quires.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning according to Asās al-Balāghah: the banāʾiq are the seams or stitched joins of a wide shirt (qamīṣ wāsiʿ al-banāʾiq), also described as its dakhārīṣ (seam-lines) or its lining (libn). To banāʾiq a garment is to sew its seams; to banāʾiq a book is to bind or stitch its quires together.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorical and scholastic extensions: (1) To banāʾiq speech (banāʾiq kalāmahu) is to gather it, arrange it, and set it in order — a rhetorical metaphor from the stitching of seams. (2) To banāʾiq a book (banāʾiq al-kitāb) is to close, finish, or dismiss it — a usage attested as a dialectal variant of nabaqa, meaning to conclude or set aside. (3) The phrase 'if you finish reading the book, then banāʾiq it and do not leave it un-banāʾiqed' carries the sense of sealing, concluding, or putting the matter to rest.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: بَنَّقَ الكِتَابَ: لُغَةٌ فِي نَبَّقَهُ. وَبَنَّقَ كَلَامَهُ: جَمَعَهُ وَسَوَّاهُ، وَمِنْهُ بَنَائِقُ القَمِيصِ أَيْ جَمْعُ شَيْءٍ. وَالبَنِيقَةُ: خِيَاطَةُ القَمِيصِ، وَالجَمْعُ بَنَائِقُ.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: To banāʾiq a book is a dialectal variant of to nabaqahu (to bind or close it). To banāʾiq one's speech is to gather it and set it in order, whence the banāʾiq of a shirt, meaning the gathering of something. The banīqah is the stitching of a shirt, and its plural is banāʾiq.

ROOT بنه   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin is a toponymic designation: a village in Egypt renowned for its honey, which the Prophet Muhammad blessed. It lacks a broader physical or verbal root meaning in the classical lexicons.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: A proper noun referring to a specific village in Egypt, pronounced Binhā (with a kasrah on the bāʾ and sukūn on the nūn), though later pronounced Banhā (with a fatḥah on the bāʾ). It is literally the name of that locality.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: No metaphorical, philosophical, or theological usages are attested; the term remains a strict toponym with no extended abstract applications.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: بِنْهَا، بكسر الباء وسكون النون، قرية من قرى مصر، بارك النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم في عسلها؛ والناس اليوم يفتحون الباء.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Binhā, with a kasrah on the bāʾ and a sukūn on the nūn, is a village in Egypt whose honey the Prophet, peace be upon him, blessed; today people pronounce it Banhā with a fatḥah on the bāʾ.

ROOT بوع   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the spatial extent of the two outstretched hands, i.e., the distance between the two palms when the arms are fully extended; from this concrete measure of bodily span, the root extends to the act of measuring by that span and to the stretching forth of the forelegs in a beast's stride.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning according to Asās al-Balāghah is the act of measuring a garment or the like by the bāʿ (the span of the outstretched arms), analogous to dharʿahu when one measures it by the dhiraʿ (cubit); likewise, the bāʿ of a camel or horse is the extension of its forelegs in walking, and a horse is described as ṭabīʿ bāʿ or baʿīd al-khaṭw when its stride is long.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: The root is applied metaphorically and scholastically to the extension of a beast's forelegs in motion (bāʿa al-baʿīr wa-l-faras wa-tabawwaʿa idhā madda bāʿahu fī sayrihi), to a she-camel as bāʿiʿah and she-camels as bawāʿiʿ when they stretch their forelegs widely in stride, and to the temporal or transactional sense in the phrase mā bīʿat hādhihi al-thiyāb ḥattā bīʿat (these garments were not sold until they were sold), where the form is used in a derived commercial context. The root also appears in the expression kam bawʿ thawbika wa-kam dharʿ thawbika (how many spans is your garment and how many cubits?), indicating measurement by bodily span.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: البَاعُ والبَوْعُ والبُوع: مَسافةُ ما بين الكفَّيْن إِذا بسَطْتهما؛ والباعُ: تَبَوُّعُ البعير والفرس في سيرهما إذا مدَّا أَيديهما.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: The bāʿ, bawʿ, and būʿ: the distance between the two palms when they are outstretched; and the bāʿ: the stretching forth of a camel and a horse in their gait when they extend their forelegs.

ROOT بيض   stratum=MA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) of the root ب-ي-ض is the color white, understood as the luminous, unmixed, and manifest opposite of black, predicated of animals, plants, and all entities susceptible to coloration.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal, sensory reality (Ḥaqīqah) of البياض is the color white itself, the quality of an object that is أبيض (white). It is a perceptible accident (ʿaraḍ) subsisting in bodies, and its verbal noun denotes the state or instance of being white.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorically and scholastically, whiteness is transferred to signify moral and spiritual purity, honor, and excellence, because the Arabs deemed white the most noble of colors. Thus, a person free of defects is said to be 'white of color' (أبيض اللون). In Quranic discourse, the whitening of faces on the Day of Judgment is a metaphor for joy, divine pleasure, and salvation, while blackening denotes grief and punishment. The dual form 'الأبيضان' metaphorically designates fat and youth, or milk and water, as the two white substances essential to life and sustenance. The active participle 'بائض' (egg-laying) is applied to birds, and 'بيوض' to a hen that lays many eggs, extending the root to the production of eggs, which are themselves white.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: البَيَاضُ: ضِدُّ السَّوَادِ، يَكُونُ فِي الحَيَوَانِ وَالنَّبَاتِ وَغَيْرِهِمَا مِمَّا يَقْبَلُهُ. وَالأَبْيَضُ: عِرْقٌ سُمِّيَ بِهِ لِكَوْنِهِ أَبْيَضَ. وَالبَيْضُ: مَعْرُوفٌ، وَبَاضَتِ الدَّجَاجَةُ تَبِيضُ بَيْضًا.

Al-Abyaḍ (the white one) is a vein named thus because of its whiteness. Al-Bayḍ (eggs) is well-known, and the hen lays eggs (bāḍat tabīḍu bayḍan).

ROOT جاز   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin of the root جاز is the act of traversing, passing through, or crossing over a boundary, barrier, or medium—whether a road, a valley, a river, or a choking obstruction in the throat—such that the traverser moves from one side, state, or condition to another.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is to pass or cross over something, as in crossing a road (جاز الطريق), traversing a valley (جاز الوادي), or passing through water (جاز الماء). -- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorically and scholastically, the root extends to: (1) the permissibility or validity of a thing (جواز), as in a legally or rationally permissible act (جائز), meaning it is not obstructed by prohibition or impossibility; (2) the granting of permission or authorization (إجازة), as in licensing a narrator, a physician, or a student, i.e., allowing them to pass beyond a boundary of qualification; (3) the execution or carrying out of a command or contract (إجازة العقد), meaning its effective passage into validity; (4) in theology and philosophy, the contingent or possible (جائز) in contrast to the necessary (واجب) and the impossible (ممتنع), denoting that whose existence or non-existence is not intrinsically obstructed; (5) the penetration of an idea or meaning through the mind (جاز المعنى في ذهنه), i.e., it passed through and was apprehended.

-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: No attested metaphorical, philosophical, or theological usage is recorded in the classical lexica; the root remains confined to its concrete physical and anatomical senses, with no extension into abstract, scholastic, or metaphysical domains.

ROOT جمعل   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root ج-م-ع-ل designates a bulky, aged, and physically coarsened entity — a creature or beast whose body has become thick, gnarled, and worn by time. Its foundational physical image is that of a heavy, hoary mass, whether a hyena (a stocky, coarse-haired predator) or an aged she-camel (a large, decrepit beast). The quadriliteral form جُمَعْلِيلَة carries an intensive diminutive-augmentative force, signaling a creature marked by both corpulence and senescence.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal, physical referent is twofold: (1) the hyena (al-ḍabʿ), so named for its bulky, coarse, and ungainly body; and (2) an aged, decrepit she-camel (al-nāqa al-harima), whose frame has grown heavy and whose strength has decayed with advanced age. In both cases, the term denotes a concrete, observable animal whose physical constitution is characterized by mass, coarseness, and the ravages of time.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: No established metaphorical, philosophical, or theological usage is attested in the classical lexicographical tradition. The term remains confined to its zoological and pastoral-literal domain. Any extended application — e.g., to a person of gross and aged constitution — would be a secondary, colloquial extension rather than a recognized majāz in the scholastic or rhetorical canon. Al-Zamakhsharī's Asās al-Balāghah does not record it, and Al-Rāghib's Al-Mufradāt does not treat it, confirming its status as a rare, concrete quadriliteral noun rather than a root with abstract or figurative derivations.
