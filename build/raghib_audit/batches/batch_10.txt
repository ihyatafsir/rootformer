ROOT قهقم   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) centers on massive, engulfing bulk — specifically the image of a creature or beast of enormous size that swallows and devours everything, and by extension the towering, raging male camel (al-faḥl al-ḍakhm al-mughtalim). The quadriliteral reduplicative form (القَهْقَمّ) itself iconically reinforces the sense of heaving, repetitive engulfment.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning, per Lisān al-ʿArab, is: a being or beast that swallows everything (al-ladhī yabtaliʿu kulla shayʾ); and a huge, raging stallion-camel (al-faḥl al-ḍakhm al-mughtalim). Abū ʿAmr records القَهْقَبّ and القَهْقَمّ as synonyms for the enormous camel (al-jamal al-ḍakhm).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Not attested in Al-Mufradāt or Asās al-Balāghah. In scholastic and rhetorical usage, the term may be extended metaphorically (majāz) to denote any person or entity of insatiable, all-consuming appetite or overwhelming, destructive force — e.g., a tyrant, a devouring army, or an intellect that absorbs all knowledge. The reduplicative quadriliteral pattern (فَعْلَلّ) carries an intensifying, hyperbolic force (mubālaghah), making the majāz extension natural in classical Arabic rhetoric.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: القِهْقَمُّ: الذي يبتلع كل شيء. والقَهْقَمُ: الفحل الضخم المغتلم. وقال أبو عمرو: القَهْقَبُّ والقَهْقَمُّ: الجمل الضخم.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Al-qihqamm: the one who swallows everything. Al-qahqam: the huge, raging stallion-camel. Abū ʿAmr said: al-qahqabb and al-qahqamm: the enormous camel.

ROOT كبه   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root ك ب ه (k-b-h) is not attested in the classical lexical tradition as an independent root with a stable physical origin (aṣl al-wadʿ). Its sole documented occurrence is a dialectal phonetic variant in which the initial ج (jīm) of جَبْهة (forehead) is shifted toward the kāf's articulation point, yielding كَبْهة. The root therefore has no autonomous ontological core; it is a phonological reflex of ج ب ه (j-b-h), whose foundational physical referent is the broad, flat frontal surface of the face.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is the forehead (al-jabha), specifically a broad, wide forehead (ʿarīḍ al-jabha). The form كَبْهة is not an independent lexical item but a dialectal pronunciation of جَبْهة, produced by fronting the jīm from its palatal articulation point toward the kāf's velar articulation point. This substitution is attested among certain Arab tribes and was recorded by Sībawayh alongside six other letters subject to analogous shifts.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: No metaphorical, philosophical, or theological extension is attested for this root in the classical sources. The only contextual usage is the Prophetic description of the Dajjāl as a man of broad forehead (ʿarīḍ al-kabha), where the dialectal form كَبْهة substitutes for جَبْهة. The term carries no abstract, ethical, or metaphysical signification; it remains strictly a physiognomic descriptor within an eschatological narrative.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: الكَبْهةُ: لغةٌ في الجَبْهةِ، وهي الجبهةُ العريضةُ؛ أَخرجَ الجيمَ بين مَخرجِها ومَخرجِ الكافِ، وهي لغةٌ لقومٍ من العرب، ذكرها سيبويه مع ستةِ أحرفٍ.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Al-kabha: a dialectal variant of al-jabha (the forehead), denoting a broad forehead; the jīm is articulated between its own point of articulation and that of the kāf. It is a dialect of certain Arab tribes, recorded by Sībawayh alongside six other letters.

-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: To sew or stitch one piece of leather to another; to join the two edges of a waterskin or the two lips of an animal by a ring; by extension, to join letters to one another in writing, and to set down words in a written line.

Thus the speech of God is called kitāb though it is not written, and the term kitāb is applied to the revealed scripture, to the sheet containing writing, and to the act of writing itself. The root also yields the sense of prescription, decree, and inscribing upon the self, as in the theological notion of God's writing deeds upon the servant.

ROOT كدا   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the retardation, delay, or sluggishness of a natural process — specifically, the tardiness of the earth in bringing forth its vegetation, such that the soil remains barren or slow to sprout despite its latent fertility.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning, per Lisān al-ʿArab, is the delayed or sluggish emergence of vegetation from the earth: كَدَت الأَرض تَكْدو كَدْواً وكُدُوًّا, فهي كاديةٌ — 'The earth was slow to vegetate, hence it is kādiyah (sluggish/barren in sprouting).' The concrete referent is a tract of land whose herbage is late in appearing.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: By metaphorical extension (majāz), the root's semantic nucleus of 'sluggishness in bringing forth' is transferred from the soil to any process, faculty, or agent whose productive or cognitive output is retarded. In scholastic and philosophical usage, it may describe the tardiness of a faculty (e.g., a sluggish intellect or a delayed apprehension), the inertia of a substance in actualizing its potential, or the reluctance of an agent to effect its proper operation. The majāz preserves the literal image of a fertile ground that withholds its yield, mapping it onto ontological or epistemological delay.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: كَدَت الأَرض تَكْدو كَدْواً وكُدُوًّا، فهي كاديةٌ إِذا أَبطأَ نباتها.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: The earth was slow to vegetate with a kadw or kudūw, so it is kādiyah when its vegetation is tardy.

ROOT لشش   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root لشش denotes lightness, agility, and rapid, unencumbered motion. Its foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the quality of being light in weight or movement, often applied to a person's constitution or gait. It is a rare root in Arabic, with the sequence of ش after ل being uncommon, as noted by classical philologists.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning according to Asās al-Balāghah is 'lightness' or 'agility' in a person, as in رجل لَشْلاشٌ (a light, agile man). It also denotes the rapid, repeated movement or trembling associated with lightness, as in اللَّشْلَشَةُ (frequent, quick motion).

ROOT ما   stratum=Ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root is not a trilateral lexical root but a primitive particle (ḥarf) whose foundational linguistic function is referential indeterminacy: it designates an unspecified entity, quantity, or proposition, and from this core radiate the functions of negation, interrogation, conditionality, relative connection, and generic predication.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: In its literal, non-metaphorical usage, ما is a particle of negation (ḥarf nafy), as in ما فعل (he did not do); it may also function as a relative pronoun equivalent to الذي (that which), as a conditional particle, as an expression covering all categories of the indefinite (ʿibārah ʿan jamīʿ anwāʿ al-nakirah), as a substitute for مَنْ (who), and as a particle of interrogation (istifhām). In certain dialectal or phonological contexts, the final alif may be replaced by hāʾ, yielding مَهْ.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Scholastic and philosophical usage extends ما beyond simple negation or relativization into the domain of logical quantification and ontological predication. In kalām and falsafa, ما functions as a tool of definitional inquiry (mā al-ḥaqīqiyyah) and as a quantifier over quiddities, allowing the theologian or philosopher to ask what a thing is in its essence. In grammar and uṣūl al-fiqh, it is treated as a general particle (ḥarf ʿāmm) whose scope may encompass all indefinite categories, and its conditional usage generates universal propositions. Its interrogative force is also employed in dialectical theology to pose questions of essence and existence, while its negative force is used to deny attributes or propositions without implying a positive counterpart.

-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Mā: a particle of negation; it may also mean 'that which'; it may denote conditionality; it may express all categories of the indefinite; it may stand in the place of 'who'; it may function as a particle of interrogation; and the alif may be replaced by hāʾ, giving māh.

ROOT مدن   stratum=Ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the act of settling and taking up residence in a place, from which the noun for a settlement or city derives. Ibn Manẓūr notes that the verb مَدَنَ بِالمَكَانِ (madana bi-l-makān) meaning 'to dwell in a place' is a dead verb (فِعْلٌ مُمَات), preserved chiefly in the derived noun المَدِينَة (al-madīnah).
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is a place of residence and settlement, i.e., a city or town. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī reports that some hold المَدِينَة to be of the pattern فَعِيلَة (faʿīlah), with its plural مَدَن (madan), while others treat the mīm as augmentative, making it مَفْعِلَة (mafʿilah) from دِنْتُ (dintu), meaning 'I was possessed or ruled.' Ibn Manẓūr records its plurals as مَدَائِن (madāʾin), مُدْن (mudn), and مُدُن (mudun).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: In scholastic and theological usage, المَدِينَة denotes the polity or community settled in a city, not merely its physical structures. The Qurʾānic phrase وَمَنْ أَهْلِ المَدِينَةِ مَرَدُوا عَلَى النِّفَاقِ (Q 9:101) refers to the hypocrites among the city's inhabitants, while وَجَاءَ مِنْ أَقْصَا المَدِينَةِ رَجُلٌ يَسْعَى (Q 36:20) and وَدَخَلَ المَدِينَةَ عَلَى حِينِ غَفْلَةٍ مِنْ أَهْلِهَا (Q 28:15) employ the term for the urban settlement as a social and political entity. The etymological debate over whether the mīm is radical or augmentative reflects a broader lexical ambiguity: if from دِنْتُ, the city is conceptually that which is possessed and governed; if from مَدَنَ, it is that in which one settles.

ROOT مقق   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is extreme elongation combined with extreme thinness — a stretched, attenuated bodily frame, and by extension the act of drawing out or extracting something entirely from its container.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: In its literal physical sense, مَقَق denotes excessive length conjoined with fineness or slenderness; a man is described as أَمَقّ and a woman as مَقَّاء, and a horse as أَشَقّ أَمَقّ. It also denotes the complete extraction of marrow from a bone (تَمَقَّقْتُ ما في العظم) and the draining of milk from the udder by a young camel (تَمَقَّقَ الفصيل ما في الضرع).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: By metaphorical extension, the root is applied to a person who speaks from the deepest part of his throat (مُقَامِق), i.e., with an affected, strained, or hollow vocalization. It is also used in the idiomatic imprecation مَقَّ الله عيني, meaning 'May God pluck out my eye,' where the physical act of extraction is transferred to a curse of removal or deprivation. The elongation sense may further shade into a pejorative description of excessive, ungainly tallness.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: المَقَقُ: الطول عامة، وقيل: هو الطول الفاحش في دقة. ورجل أمق وامرأة مقاء. وتمققت ما في العظم: استخرجته كله. وفلان مقامق: يتكلم بأقصى حلقه.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Al-Maqaq denotes length in general; it is also said to be excessive length combined with slenderness. A man is described as amaqq and a woman as maqqā'. TamAqqaqtu mā fī al-'aẓm means 'I extracted everything within the bone.' Fulān muqāmiq means 'so-and-so speaks from the deepest part of his throat.'
