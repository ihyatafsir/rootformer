ROOT مكد   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the notion of fixed, unmoving persistence in a place or state — a thing that stays put and does not shift, flow away, or dissipate. From this concrete sense of stationary continuance, the root extends to denote anything enduring, perpetual, or unceasing, such as water that remains and does not dry up.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is: to remain, stay, abide, or dwell in a place (مَكَدَ بالمكان يَمْكُدُ مُكُوداً: أَقام به). It is synonymous with ثَكَمَ (to stay) and رَكَدَ (to be still, motionless). Applied to water, ماءٌ ماكِدٌ denotes water that is permanent, unfailing, and never ceasing — standing water that persists rather than draining or evaporating.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: By extension from the literal sense of stationary persistence, the root is applied metaphorically to anything characterized by uninterrupted continuance: enduring states, perpetual conditions, and unceasing phenomena. The participle ماكِد thus describes not merely what is physically still, but what is constant and everlasting — e.g., water that is دائم (perpetual), and by analogy any quality, blessing, or condition that abides without interruption. The scholastic usage preserves the core image of something that does not move away or come to an end.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: مَكَدَ بالمكان يَمْكُدُ مُكُوداً: أَقام به؛ وثَكَمَ يَثْكُم مثله، ورَكَدَ رُكوداً. وماءٌ ماكِدٌ: دائمٌ.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: مَكَدَ in a place, يَمْكُدُ مُكُوداً: he stayed or dwelt there; ثَكَمَ يَثْكُم is the same, and رَكَدَ رُكوداً [is likewise]. And ماءٌ ماكِدٌ means perpetual, unfailing water.

ROOT ملز   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) of the root م-ل-ز is the act of slipping away, escaping, or quietly departing from a place, grasp, or state. It denotes a subtle, often elusive motion of withdrawal or release, frequently paired with its phonetic sibling م-ل-س (malasa), which carries the same semantic field of slipping out or slipping away.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is: a thing slipped away from me, departed, or went off (مَلَزَ الشيءُ عَنِّي); and one escaped or got free from a matter (امَّلَزَ من الأمر), or extracted oneself from it (تَمَلَّزَ من الأمر). The transitive form مَلَّزْتُه means: I caused it to slip away or made it depart.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: In metaphorical and scholastic usage, the root is applied to the subtle evasion of an obligation, the quiet withdrawal from a commitment, or the eluding of a grasp—whether physical, legal, or moral. It conveys a stealthy disentanglement rather than a forceful escape, and in theological or ethical discourse it may describe the soul's slipping away from a virtuous state or the intellect's evasion of a conclusive proof. The paired root م-ل-س is used interchangeably in classical texts for the same range of literal and figurative slipping.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: مَلَزَ الشيءُ عَنِّي مَلْزاً وامَّلَزَ ومَلَّزَ: ذهب. وتَمَلَّزَ من الأَمر تَمَلُّزاً وتَمَلَّسَ تَمَلُّساً: خرج منه. وامَّلَزَ من الأَمر وامَّلَسَ إِذا انفلت. وقد مَلَّزْتُه ومَلَّسْتُه إِذا فعلت به ذلك.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: The thing slipped away from me (malaza), and it escaped (immallaza) and slipped away (mallaza): it departed. And he extracted himself from the matter (tamallaza) and slipped out (tamallasa): he exited from it. And he escaped from the matter (immallaza) and slipped free (immallasa) when he got loose. And I caused it to slip away (mallaztuhu) and made it slip free (mallastuhu) when I did that to it.

ROOT منن   stratum=MA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the act of cutting or severing a rope or cord, and the consequent state of a rope that is worn, frayed, and weakened to the point of breaking. From this concrete sense of physical debilitation and severance, the root branches into two major semantic streams: (1) the bestowal of a heavy favor or benefit that 'cuts' or burdens the recipient with obligation, and (2) the notion of a weak, severed, or depleted thing, whether a rope, a person's strength, or a garment's fabric.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: According to Asās al-Balāghah, the literal physical meaning is a rope (ḥabl) that is weak, worn out, and severed (manīn), and the act of cutting (mann) a rope. It also denotes the physical act of a person being weakened or depleted, as in 'manat-hu al-manūn' (the fates cut him off) and 'manat-hu al-safar' (travel weakened him). The literal sense also includes the physical burden of a heavy favor, as in 'manna fulānun ʿalā fulān' (a person burdened another with a favor).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorically and theologically, the root is used to denote the bestowal of a favor or blessing (niʿmah), especially from God to His servants, as in 'God conferred a favor upon the believers' (Q 3:164). This usage is considered majāz because the favor is not a physical rope but is likened to a heavy burden that 'cuts' or obligates the recipient. The term 'mannān' (the Ever-Bestowing) is a divine attribute. It also metaphorically signifies the psychological burden of a favor that is reminded of repeatedly, which is condemned in Islamic ethics. Additionally, 'manīn' is used metaphorically for a weak person or a worn-out garment, and 'manūn' (fate) is personified as that which cuts off life.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: مَنَّهُ يَمُنُّهُ مَنّاً: قطعه. والمَنِينُ: الحبل الضعيف. وحبل مَنينٌ: مقطوع. والمنة: النعمة الثقيلة. والمنان: الكثير المنن. والمنون: القطع، والمنية: الموت.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Mannahu yamunnuhu mannan: he cut it. Al-manīn: the weak rope. A rope that is manīn: severed. Al-minnah: the heavy favor. Al-mannān: the one who bestows many favors. Al-manūn: cutting, and al-maniyyah: death.

ROOT مهر   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin centers on the act of giving or assigning a dower (ṣadāq) to a woman, and by extension, the notion of a contractual payment that seals a union; from this concrete transactional sense, the root branches into the idea of skill or mastery, conceived as a 'price' or 'acquisition' of proficiency, and into the concrete noun for a camel's nose-ring (mihār), a device that controls and directs.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning according to Asās al-Balāghah is the dower given to a woman (mahr al-marʾah: to give her the dower), and the verbal noun denotes the act of assigning or paying that dower; it also literally denotes a nose-ring (mihār) placed in the nose of a camel, and a horse possessing such a ring (faras mumhar).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorically and scholastically, the root is used for skill and mastery in a craft or activity: māhir means 'skilled, expert' (e.g., a skilled orator, a skilled swimmer), and tamahhara means 'to become proficient' or 'to swim well.' The dower is also metaphorically extended to any gift or compensation that seals a contract, and the nose-ring is metaphorically applied to any controlling or directing device. Theologically, the dower is a legal obligation in marriage, and the root's semantic shift to 'skill' is understood as a metaphorical 'price' paid for excellence.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: المَهْرُ: الصَّداق، والجمع مُهور؛ وقد مَهَرَ المرأَةَ يَمْهَرُها ويَمْهُرُها مَهْراً وأَمْهَرَها. والمَاهِرُ: الحاذق بالشيء، وقد مَهَرَ يَمْهَرُ مَهَارَةً. والمِهَارُ: عود في أنف البختي.

ROOT موا   stratum=ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root م و ا is a secondary, denominative formation from the primary root م و ه / م ي ه (water), denoting the substance of water and, by extension, anything that shares its defining property of limpid transparency and reflective clarity.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal, physical referent of the derived noun مَاوِيَّة is a mirror (مِرْآة), so named because it is fashioned from, or functions as, a polished watery surface: images are seen in it exactly as they are seen in clear, still water. A second literal application is the crystal stone (حَجَر البِلَّوْر), i.e. rock crystal, on account of its water-like translucency.
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: The scholastic and figurative extension rests on the transferred attribute of صَفَاء (limpidity) and انعكاس الصورة (image-reflection): any polished, transparent, or specular medium may be termed مَاوِيَّة by assimilation to the reflective surface of water. The lexicographers explicitly ground this majāz in the resemblance (تشبيه) of the mirror's function to that of still water, and they affirm that the medial mīm is radical (أصلية), not an augment, thereby anchoring the word in the water-root rather than treating it as a mere instrumental noun of رؤية.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: المَاوِيَّةُ: المِرْآةُ، كأَنَّها نُسِبَت إِلى الماءِ لصَفائها وأَنَّ الصُّورَ تُرى فيها كما تُرى في الماءِ الصافي، والميمُ أَصليةٌ فيها؛ وقيل: المَاوِيَّةُ حَجَرُ البِلَّوْرِ، وثلاثُ مَاوِيَّاتٍ.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.en: Al-māwiyyah: the mirror, as though it were attributed to water (al-māʾ) because of its limpidity and because images are seen in it just as they are seen in clear water; the mīm therein is radical. It is also said that al-māwiyyah is rock crystal; the plural is three māwiyyāt.

ROOT ميث   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) of the root م-ي-ث is the act of dissolving a solid substance—such as salt, bread, or clay—in water through kneading, rubbing, and maceration until it loses its solid form and becomes fully incorporated into the liquid medium.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning according to Asās al-Balāghah is twofold: (1) the softening and leveling of land, as in أرض ميثاء (soft, level ground) and أراض ميث (soft, level lands); and (2) the physical dissolution of a solid in water, as in ماث الخبز والملح والطين في الماء (he dissolved bread, salt, and clay in water) and انماث (it dissolved).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: The metaphorical and scholastic extensions of the root derive from the physical process of dissolution. In theological and philosophical discourse, the root is used to describe the annihilation of a thing's distinct essence or form through its complete absorption into another medium, akin to the dissolution of salt in water. This serves as a metaphor for the obliteration of individual identity, the merging of substances, or the total assimilation of one entity into another, often with connotations of loss of distinct existence or the subsuming of the particular into the universal.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: مَاثَ الشيءَ مَيْثاً: مَرَسَه. وماثَ المِلحَ في الماء: أَذابه؛ وكذلك الطين، وقد انماثَ. وكلُّ شيءٍ مَرَسته في الماء فذاب فيه، فقد مِثْته ومُثْته.

ROOT نبه   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the abrupt rousing of a dormant or heedless being into wakefulness and attentive presence — the transition from the inertia of sleep or negligence to active consciousness and alertness.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning, per Asās al-Balāghah, is awakening from sleep: a person wakes from his sleep (انتبه من نومه), is roused (استنبه، تنبّه), and one rouses him (نبّهه). It also denotes a person of alert, discerning mind (رجل نبيه), and to raise someone's name in renown (نبّهت باسمه: I made his name prominent).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorically and scholastically, the root extends from sensory waking to intellectual and moral awakening: to become mindful of what one had overlooked (أضلّوه نبهاً: they lost it without realizing when, until they became attentive to it); to be distinguished by nobility of intellect and character (نباهة, نبيه); and to elevate someone's reputation through public mention (تنبيه الاسم, tanwīh). -- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning, as attested in Asās al-Balāghah, is the extraction of a thorn with a pair of extractors (مِنْتَاش), or the removal of a thorn with a pair of tweezers (مِنْقَاش). -- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: The metaphorical and scholastic applications of the root extend from the physical act of extraction to intellectual and epistemic domains. In this majāz, the intellect is likened to a hand that plucks or extracts insights from the body of knowledge, mirroring the literal act of removing a thorn from flesh. Additionally, Ibn Manẓūr records a distinct nominal usage: النَّتَش (al-natash) refers to the whiteness that appears at the base of the fingernail, a physical phenomenon that may be considered a secondary literal or observational extension of the root's semantic field.

ROOT نزف   stratum=Ma
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) is the exhaustive drawing-off of a well's water, ladle by ladle, until the source is completely depleted; hence the root denotes total evacuation of a contained substance, whether water, blood, tears, or, by extension, the rational faculties.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: The literal physical meaning is to drain a well completely by drawing its water out gradually, or for a well to become so drained; it is used transitively (nazafa al-bīra) and intransitively (nazafat al-bīru), and in the passive (nuzifat). It also applies literally to the complete extraction of blood (nazafa damahu) or tears (nazafa damʿahu).
-- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: Metaphorical and scholastic usages include: (1) the depletion of intellect or understanding by intoxication, hence 'sakrān nazīf' (a drunkard whose reason has been drained) and the Qurʾānic 'lā yuṣaddaʿūna ʿanhā wa-lā yunzafūn' (they will neither suffer headache from it nor lose their minds), with the variant 'yunzafūn' derived from 'anzafū' meaning their drink is exhausted or their minds are removed; (2) the exhaustion of one's argument in disputation, as in 'nazafa al-rajulu fī al-khuṣūmah' (the man's proof was cut off); (3) the proverbial 'ajbanu min al-manzūfi ḍarṭan' (more cowardly than one whose flatulence has been drained), referring to extreme fear.

-- RECORD.ontological_core: The root's foundational physical origin (Aṣl al-Wadʿ) centers on the act of administering a substance into the mouth or throat, specifically forcing a liquid or medicine into someone's mouth, and by extension, the giving of a fee or bribe to a soothsayer to induce speech.

This usage is metaphorical because the fee is like medicine forced into the soothsayer's mouth to make him speak or prophesy. ROOT نضح   stratum=mA
-- RECORD.ontological_core: The foundational physical origin of the root نضح is the act of sprinkling or scattering water or a similar fluid in fine, dispersed droplets upon a surface, as opposed to a full, drenching pour. This core image underlies all subsequent literal and figurative applications.
-- RECORD.haqiqah_literal: According to Asās al-Balāghah, the literal meaning is the sprinkling of water upon something (نضح عليه الماء) or the sprinkling of a house with water (نضح البيت بالماء), which is characterized as رش (rashsh), i.e., a light, scattered sprinkling. -- RECORD.majaz_scholastic: In metaphorical and scholastic usage, the root extends to any subtle, non-violent diffusion or emanation, such as the gentle spread of moisture, the exudation of perspiration as a sign of bodily function, or the sprinkling of a liquid as a symbolic act of purification or blessing. It may also be applied to the diffuse radiation of light or the soft dissemination of knowledge, always retaining the nuance of fine, dispersed particles rather than a concentrated flow.
-- RECORD.canonical_definition.ar: النَّضْحُ: الرَّشُّ. نَضَحَ عَلَيْهِ المَاءَ يَنْضَحُهُ نَضْحًا، وَنَضَحَ البَيْتَ بِالمَاءِ، وَنَضَحَ جِلْدُهُ بِالعَرَقِ.
